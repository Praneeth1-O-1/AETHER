1. Purpose

AETHER produces land-cover, road and building maps from optical, radar and elevation satellite data, and keeps producing them when cloud hides the optical image. It also outputs a reliance map showing which sensor each pixel's prediction relied on. Accuracy metrics measure correctness against labels. They do not show whether prospective users find the maps plausible, whether the reliance map helps them decide what to trust, or which applications they would use it for. This study asks those questions directly.

3. Participants

  • Target: at least 30 participants (the course's recommended minimum).
  • Who: students and professionals likely to use land-cover data: remote sensing / GIS, civil and urban planning, agriculture and environment, and AI/ML students as a contrasting group. Profile questions A1–A4 record background and familiarity.
  • Recruitment: department mailing lists, GIS and remote-sensing student groups, and classmates from related courses. Participation is voluntary and unpaid.
  • Exclusion: members of Group 108 and anyone who has worked on AETHER.

4. Materials

Six tiles from the held-out test split (locations never seen in training): three clear and three cloud-covered, chosen to span different land-cover types and continents. For each tile the form shows:

  1. the Sentinel-2 true-colour image;
  2. AETHER's predicted land-cover map with a class legend;
  3. for the trust questions (Section C), the same map shown first alone and then with its reliance map (red = optical, green = radar, blue = elevation), with a one-sentence explanation.

Tiles are selected by a fixed rule (first three clear and first three cloudy test tiles in sorted order whose labelled area exceeds 80%) rather than hand-picked, so the materials don't favour good predictions.

The six tile images are generated on Kaggle from the epoch-41 model (the local machine is not used for model runs). Ground-truth panels are not shown to participants. The form tests user perception, not label agreement.

5. Procedure

  1. The participant opens the Google Form (about 12–15 minutes) and reads the information sheet and consent statement (Section 7).
  2. Section A: background and familiarity.
  3. Section B: for each of the six tiles, rate how well (clear) or how plausibly (cloudy) the map matches the image. Tile order is fixed to keep the form simple. Clear tiles come first so participants learn the legend before facing cloud.
  4. Section C: one tile shown without, then with, its reliance map. Trust is rated before and after (C1, C2), followed by three statements about the reliance map.
  5. Sections D–E: usefulness, applications, likelihood of use, and two open questions.

6. Measures

All rating items use a 1–5 scale with labelled endpoints. The full question list, with codes, is in questions.csv. Each Google Form question title must start with its code in brackets (e.g. [B1] …) so the analysis script can match responses.

8. Analysis Plan

Run python analyze_user_study.py responses.csv --out results/ on the Google Forms CSV export. The script reports:

  • Descriptives per item: n, mean, median, standard deviation and % agreeing (4–5). Medians are primary, because the data are ordinal.
  • H2: Wilcoxon signed-rank test, C1 vs C2 (paired), with effect size r = |z| / √n. α = 0.05.
  • H1: per-participant mean of clear tiles vs cloudy tiles, Wilcoxon signed-rank.
  • Reliability: Cronbach's α for the map-quality, reliance-map and usefulness item groups. Values ≥ 0.7 support averaging the items.
  • Applications: counts for D3.
  • Open answers: E1/E2 coded into themes by two team members independently, with disagreements resolved by discussion; report the themes with counts and short quotes.
  • A diverging stacked bar chart of all rating items (results/likert.png).

Limitations to report: convenience sample, mostly students; a single tile for the trust comparison; fixed item order (order effects are not controlled); and perceived plausibility is not the same as measured accuracy.
